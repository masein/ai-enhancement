# History & Archaeology Evaluation Rubric

## Task

Score **ONE answer to ONE History & Archaeology question from 0–4**.

The question metadata establishes the relevant domain, intent, difficulty, historical/archaeological context, and expected reasoning style.

Accept different valid historical interpretations, causal explanations, periodizations, archaeological interpretations, and source evaluations where the evidence permits them. Do not require a particular historiographical school, famous scholar, artifact typology, or wording unless specified. Do not reward unnecessary length, fact dumping, jargon, or citations.

## Critical Error

If `critical_history_archaeology_error` applies:

**score = 0**

Otherwise evaluate the applicable criteria.

A critical History & Archaeology error is a materially false central historical, chronological, archaeological, evidential, or causal claim that fundamentally invalidates the answer or reverses the substantive conclusion.

Do **not** classify as critical minor date slips that do not affect chronology, small arithmetic errors that do not change the conclusion, minor terminology mistakes, defensible alternative interpretations, legitimate historiographical disagreement, reasonable uncertainty, cautious causal claims, minor omissions, or reasonable approximations.

## Score Anchors

### 4
The answer is historically and archaeologically accurate, directly addresses the question, establishes the relevant chronology, contextualizes evidence appropriately, evaluates source or archaeological limitations, distinguishes evidence from interpretation, considers material alternative explanations, and reaches a well-supported conclusion. For source questions, it evaluates provenance, audience, purpose, and evidential limits. For causal questions, it identifies mechanisms and distinguishes triggers from deeper causes where relevant. For archaeological questions, it correctly reasons about context, stratigraphy, dating, preservation, and site formation. For contested questions, it calibrates uncertainty and represents competing interpretations fairly.

### 3
The answer is substantially correct and useful but contains one meaningful omission, minor historical imprecision, limited source criticism, non-central chronology error, or insufficient treatment of alternatives or uncertainty. The central interpretation remains sound.

### 2
The answer is partly correct or directionally useful but has important omissions, weak contextualization, significant chronological problems, weak source criticism, archaeological overinterpretation, or insufficient causal reasoning. Meaningful correction would be required.

### 1
The answer contains a substantial historical or archaeological misunderstanding, major non-critical chronology error, inappropriate causal argument, weak source analysis, or badly incomplete interpretation, but does not meet the critical-error threshold.

### 0
Critical History & Archaeology error, no answer, off-topic answer, unrelated fabrication, or equivalent fundamental failure.

## Criterion Application

Evaluate only criteria relevant to the question. A diplomatic-history question need not discuss radiocarbon dating. A stratigraphy problem need not discuss historiography. A primary-source interpretation need not contain quantitative analysis. Conditional criteria should not penalize answers for omitting irrelevant material. However, when a conditional issue is essential to correctness, failure on that issue should affect the score.

## Important Evaluation Principles

### Chronology is foundational
An explanation based on events occurring in the wrong order is not historically valid.

### Chronology is not causation
Event A preceding Event B does not by itself establish that A caused B.

### Context matters
Historical actors, institutions, and texts should be interpreted within their historical setting.

### Avoid anachronism
Do not project later categories, institutions, technologies, or identities backward without justification.

### Avoid teleology
Historical outcomes should not be presented as inevitable merely because they happened.

### Sources are produced artifacts
Written records reflect authorship, audience, purpose, institutional setting, information access, and preservation.

### Bias is not binary
A source can be interested, partisan, propagandistic, or limited and still provide useful evidence. The task is to determine what it can support.

### Surviving evidence is selective
Archives and archaeological assemblages are incomplete.

### Silence requires interpretation
Absence of evidence may reflect true absence, poor preservation, limited sampling, archival destruction, or non-documentation. In a well-sampled context, however, absence can itself become informative.

### Primary sources are not automatically superior
Primary and secondary sources answer different questions; primary status does not confer automatic reliability.

### Historiography matters
Historical knowledge can change when new evidence appears, old evidence is reinterpreted, methods change, or research questions change.

### Written evidence is not automatically superior to material evidence
Texts and archaeology can corroborate, contradict, constrain, or illuminate different aspects of the past.

### Archaeological context matters
An artifact removed from context loses substantial interpretive information.

### Dating has uncertainty
Scientific dates should be interpreted with their ranges, dated material, context, association with the event, and assumptions.

### Material culture does not map directly onto ethnicity
Artifact style alone rarely establishes social identity.

### Site formation matters
Archaeological deposits reflect both past human behavior and subsequent natural and cultural processes.

### Preservation is uneven
Organic and inorganic materials survive differently across environments.

### Sampling matters
Survey and excavation reveal samples of past landscapes and deposits, not complete past reality.

### Historical statistics require contextualization
Definitions, boundaries, currencies, measurements, and record-keeping systems can change across time.

### Global history requires regional balance
Do not treat one region's chronology or institutions as the default model for all societies.

### Comparative history requires comparable cases
Similar labels do not guarantee comparable institutions or processes.

### Environmental factors do not mechanically determine history
Environment interacts with institutions, technology, culture, political power, and demographic conditions.

### Present moral evaluation and historical explanation are distinct
It is possible to evaluate past actions ethically while still explaining them using historically appropriate context.

### Multiple interpretations can be legitimate
Where evidence is incomplete, reward well-supported alternatives that fit the supplied evidence.

### No fact-list worship
Long lists of dates, rulers, battles, dynasties, or artifacts do not substitute for analysis.

### No theory worship
Historiographical frameworks are analytical tools, not answers by themselves.

### No citation worship
Citations do not compensate for incorrect chronology, fabricated evidence, or weak historical reasoning.

## Primary-Source Answers
Strong answers should consider authorship, date, audience, purpose, genre, historical context, access to information, omissions, corroboration, source dependence, and what the source can support. Do not reward merely declaring a source "biased."

## Causal Answers
Strong answers should establish sequence, identify mechanisms, distinguish trigger from deeper cause, consider structural and contingent factors, consider alternatives, and avoid inevitability.

## Comparative Answers
Strong answers should identify relevant dimensions of comparison, preserve regional context, recognize different chronologies, identify common mechanisms and meaningful differences, and avoid assuming one case is the standard.

## Quantitative-History Answers
Strong answers should use correct units and denominators, identify changes in definitions, recognize missing data, distinguish stocks from flows, interpret magnitude historically, and avoid false precision where relevant.

## Archaeological Answers
Strong answers should consider context, provenience, association, stratigraphy, preservation, disturbance, sampling, dating, and alternative interpretations where relevant. Do not infer complex social conclusions directly from isolated artifacts.

## Dating Answers
Strong answers should identify what was dated, the dating method, the relationship between sample and event, uncertainty/range, possible contamination or reuse, and compatibility with stratigraphy where relevant.

## Mixed-Evidence Answers
Strong answers should explain how texts, archaeology, environmental evidence, demographic data, oral history, and quantitative evidence support, contradict, or constrain one another. Do not automatically privilege one evidence type.
