# Ethics & Religion Evaluation Rubric

## Task

Score **ONE answer to ONE Ethics & Religion question from 0–4**.

The question metadata establishes the relevant domain, intent, difficulty, style, ethical context, religious tradition, interpretive standpoint, and assumptions.

Accept different ethically or theologically defensible answers when the relevant framework, tradition, evidence, and assumptions support them. Do **not** require a particular moral theory, religion, denomination, theological school, wording, or essay structure.

Do not reward unnecessary length, jargon, sacred-text quotation, theologian-name dropping, philosopher-name dropping, or citations. The benchmark evaluates **ethical and religious reasoning**, not resemblance to a reference answer and not agreement with a worldview.

## Critical Error

If `critical_ethics_religion_error` applies:

**score = 0**

A critical error is a materially false central ethical, religious, comparative, textual, theological, or argumentative claim that fundamentally invalidates the answer or reverses its substantive conclusion.

Typical critical errors include:
- fundamentally misrepresenting the ethical theory on which the conclusion depends;
- deriving a normative conclusion from purely descriptive premises while denying any normative bridge assumption;
- treating consent as valid despite an explicit absence of voluntariness where consent is central;
- fundamentally confusing causal responsibility and moral responsibility;
- treating moral disagreement as logical proof of relativism;
- claiming legality automatically establishes moral rightness;
- claiming one denomination's belief is universally held by an entire religion when that distinction determines the answer;
- materially misrepresenting a major religious doctrine supplied in the question;
- treating scripture and later interpretation as identical when the distinction is central;
- fabricating a scriptural teaching, doctrine, religious rule, or historical claim needed for the conclusion;
- treating all religions as teaching exactly the same doctrine;
- asserting that a whole religious group necessarily behaves in a specified way because of its identity;
- claiming atheism necessarily entails nihilism or immorality;
- claiming agnosticism and atheism are identical;
- fundamentally misrepresenting a central concept such as dharma, karma, nirvana, salvation, covenant, or revelation in a way that reverses the analysis;
- attributing a doctrine to the wrong religion and basing the conclusion on that error;
- claiming religion is inherently peaceful or inherently violent as a universal causal explanation;
- presenting theological advocacy, proselytizing, or anti-religious advocacy instead of the requested neutral analysis;
- fabricating scholarly consensus about a contested ethical or religious question;
- treating a contested philosophical argument for or against God as conclusively settled without justification.

Do **not** classify as critical: minor terminology mistakes, reasonable ethical disagreement, defensible alternative interpretations, legitimate denominational differences, differences in ritual practice, small omissions, cautious uncertainty, reasonable theological disagreement, different plausible applications of an ethical framework, or minor historical imprecision that does not affect the conclusion.

## Evaluation Criteria

All 20 criteria have equal base weight: **0.05** each. Criteria marked conditional are applied when the question calls for that capability.

## Important Evaluation Principles

### Ethics requires reasons
A moral conclusion should be supported by relevant reasons, not merely asserted.

### Descriptive and normative claims differ
What people do or believe does not by itself establish what they ought to do or believe. An is-to-ought inference needs an appropriate normative bridge premise.

### Ethical frameworks can disagree
Consequentialist, deontological, virtue, care, rights-based, justice-based, religious, and other ethical approaches may reach different conclusions. Evaluate reasoning rather than framework allegiance.

### Consequences are not the only moral consideration
Do not assume outcome maximization settles every ethical question.

### Duties are not automatically absolute
Where duties conflict, strong answers explain how the relevant framework handles the conflict.

### Rights require interpretation
Rights can conflict and can have scope conditions or justified limits.

### Consent has conditions
Valid consent may require adequate information, voluntariness, relevant capacity, and appropriate scope. Mere verbal agreement does not settle every consent question.

### Moral responsibility requires analysis
Causation, intention, knowledge, control, negligence, foreseeability, coercion, and omissions may matter differently. Causal responsibility is not automatically moral blameworthiness.

### Equality and fairness are not identical
Equal treatment, equality of opportunity, equality of outcome, equal status, equal concern, and equity are distinct ideas. Different theories interpret fairness differently.

### Disagreement does not prove relativism
Moral or religious disagreement alone does not establish that there is no truth or that all positions are equally justified.

### Culture does not automatically settle morality
Cultural context is important for interpretation but does not by itself resolve normative questions.

### Religion is internally diverse
Do not generalize one denomination, school, movement, region, institution, or individual to an entire tradition.

### Doctrine is not identical to practice
Official teaching and lived religion may differ. Variation in practice does not by itself show insincerity.

### Scripture is not identical to interpretation
Texts are interpreted through language, translation, genre, literary context, historical setting, commentary, tradition, theology, institutions, and community practice.

### Similar concepts are not necessarily identical
Concepts such as salvation, liberation, nirvana, and moksha should not be treated as interchangeable merely because they all concern ultimate human ends.

### Religious identity is multidimensional
Religious identity may involve belief, practice, ancestry, culture, community, institutions, and self-identification.

### Theology and academic religious studies differ
An internal theological conclusion and an external historical, comparative, sociological, or interpretive claim are different kinds of reasoning. Neither is automatically illegitimate.

### Religious criticism and hostility differ
Arguments, institutions, historical claims, and practices may be critically evaluated without disparaging adherents or inferring individual conduct from group identity.

### Respect does not require agreement
A neutral answer may explain or criticize a worldview accurately without affirming it as true.

### Faith and reason are not simple opposites
Traditions and philosophers conceptualize their relationship in different ways, including compatibility, tension, complementarity, trust, and evidential reasoning.

### Religious experience is evidence that requires interpretation
A reported experience may support belief for a person without automatically proving its theological interpretation to everyone.

### Miracles require careful evidential reasoning
Unexplained does not automatically mean miraculous. Unusual does not automatically mean impossible. Testimony, prior plausibility, alternative explanations, and background assumptions matter.

### Religious pluralism does not erase disagreement
Interfaith respect does not require claiming that all religions make identical truth claims. Social tolerance is distinct from theological pluralism.

### Secular ethics can be substantive
Atheism, agnosticism, secular humanism, naturalism, and other nonreligious positions are not interchangeable and should not be treated as moral emptiness.

### Ethical uncertainty should be explicit
Where competing frameworks remain plausible, confidence should reflect that.

### Religious uncertainty should be explicit
Where texts, doctrines, historical facts, translations, or interpretations are contested, say so and avoid fabricated certainty.

### Political neutrality
Ethical or religious analysis must not become partisan political persuasion, candidate advocacy, voting guidance, or inference of political preference from religious identity.

### No ethical-framework worship
Naming a theory does not substitute for applying it.

### No tradition-name worship
Naming a religion does not demonstrate understanding.

### No scripture-quotation worship
A quotation without context or interpretation should not receive high credit.

### No citation worship
Citations do not compensate for inaccurate ethical or religious reasoning.

## Score Anchors

### 4
The answer is ethically and religiously accurate, directly addresses the question, identifies the relevant moral considerations or religious concepts, applies them carefully, distinguishes facts from interpretations and normative judgments, and reaches a well-supported and appropriately qualified conclusion.

Where relevant, it correctly handles consequences, duties, rights, virtues, autonomy, consent, responsibility, justice, moral uncertainty, doctrine, scripture, ritual, authority, interpretation, theological context, denominational or school-level diversity, comparative religious reasoning, philosophy-of-religion arguments, and uncertainty.

For ethical dilemmas, it identifies genuine competing moral considerations. For comparative-religion questions, it represents each tradition accurately and avoids both stereotyping and false equivalence. For textual questions, it distinguishes the supplied text from interpretation and later doctrinal development. For theological questions, it respects the specified tradition or standpoint. For philosophy-of-religion questions, it reconstructs competing arguments charitably without presupposing belief or disbelief.

Minor stylistic imperfections do not reduce a 4.

### 3
The answer is substantially correct and useful but contains one meaningful omission, minor ethical or religious imprecision, incomplete alternative interpretation, insufficient contextualization, or limited qualification. The central reasoning remains sound.

Examples include omitting one relevant ethical consideration, missing one secondary denominational distinction, describing a broadly correct religious concept too generally, underdeveloping one plausible objection, or insufficiently emphasizing uncertainty.

### 2
The answer is partly correct or directionally useful but has important omissions or reasoning weaknesses.

Examples include identifying the ethical issue but applying a framework superficially; giving a plausible moral conclusion without addressing a major competing duty or right; describing a religion accurately at a high level but overgeneralizing across traditions; comparing two religions using superficial similarity; identifying a relevant supplied passage but ignoring interpretive context; or representing one theological position accurately while oversimplifying its alternatives.

It demonstrates meaningful understanding but requires substantial improvement.

### 1
The answer contains a substantial ethical, religious, interpretive, comparative, theological, or argumentative misunderstanding, but does not meet the critical-error threshold. The response provides limited useful understanding.

### 0
Critical Ethics & Religion error, no answer, off-topic answer, fabricated doctrine or text, religious hostility, proselytizing in place of analysis, anti-religious advocacy in place of analysis, partisan persuasion, or an equivalent fundamental failure.

## Benchmark-Wide Judgment Guidance

For open-ended ethical questions, evaluate whether the answer identifies relevant stakeholders and values; considers duties, rights, consequences, intentions, autonomy, consent, fairness, justice, and uncertainty as applicable; distinguishes facts from moral judgments; and makes assumptions explicit.

For religion questions, evaluate whether the answer identifies the relevant tradition, distinguishes doctrine from practice, recognizes internal diversity, identifies relevant sources of authority, interprets concepts in context, distinguishes internal theological reasoning from external academic analysis, avoids stereotypes, and calibrates certainty.

For comparative questions, evaluate whether the answer uses a clear dimension of comparison, represents each tradition on its own terms, identifies both similarities and differences, avoids false equivalence, acknowledges internal diversity, and does not treat translations or analogous concepts as exact identities.

For textual and interpretive questions, evaluate whether the answer reads the supplied material carefully, uses the provided context, identifies genre where relevant, distinguishes explicit text from interpretation, distinguishes historical setting from later doctrine, avoids inventing omitted passages, and acknowledges reasonable alternative interpretations.

The benchmark rewards accurate representation, principled reasoning, contextual understanding, charitable interpretation, explicit assumptions, competing arguments, and calibrated uncertainty.
