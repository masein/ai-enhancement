# General & Multidisciplinary Evaluation Rubric

## Task

Score **ONE answer to ONE General & Multidisciplinary question from 0–4**.

The question metadata establishes the relevant domain, intent, difficulty, style, and context.

Accept different valid explanations, interpretations, estimates, and practical approaches when they are consistent with the supplied facts and reasonably justified.

Do not require a particular wording or structure. Do not reward unnecessary length, jargon, or citations. The benchmark evaluates accurate reasoning across general and interdisciplinary problems rather than resemblance to a reference answer.

## Critical Error

If `critical_general_multidisciplinary_error` applies:

**score = 0**

A critical error is a materially false central factual, logical, quantitative, scientific, historical, geographic, causal, or evidentiary claim that fundamentally invalidates the response or reverses its substantive conclusion.

Examples include a decisive quantitative error; impossible scientific reasoning; chronology or geographic mistakes that determine the answer; treating correlation as proof of causation when central; fundamentally misreading supplied evidence; fabricating facts or evidence required for the conclusion; asserting a unique answer where the information is clearly underdetermined; or building a multidisciplinary conclusion on mutually incompatible claims.

Do **not** classify as critical: minor factual inaccuracies that do not change the conclusion, harmless rounding, small omissions, stylistic weaknesses, legitimate interpretive differences, reasonable approximations, clearly stated alternative assumptions, failure to mention a secondary consideration, a different but reasonable practical recommendation, or cautious qualification where certainty is impossible.

## Score Anchors

### 4

The answer is accurate, directly addresses the question, applies appropriate reasoning, uses relevant evidence correctly, identifies important assumptions or uncertainty where necessary, and reaches a well-supported conclusion.

For multidisciplinary questions, it connects the relevant domains coherently.

For quantitative questions, calculations and interpretations are correct.

For source or evidence questions, it appropriately evaluates strength and limitations.

For practical questions, it recognizes material objectives, constraints, risks, and trade-offs.

For underdetermined questions, it correctly identifies what cannot be concluded and what additional information would be needed.

Minor stylistic imperfections do not reduce a 4.

### 3

The answer is substantially correct and useful but contains one meaningful omission, minor factual imprecision, limited reasoning gap, incomplete qualification, or insufficient consideration of an important secondary factor.

The main conclusion remains well supported.

### 2

The answer is partly correct or directionally useful but has important omissions, weak synthesis, incomplete reasoning, insufficient evidence evaluation, questionable assumptions, or a materially incomplete interpretation.

It demonstrates some understanding but would need significant improvement.

### 1

The answer contains a substantial factual, logical, quantitative, evidentiary, or multidisciplinary misunderstanding, but does not meet the critical-error threshold.

The response provides limited useful understanding.

### 0

Critical general/multidisciplinary error, no answer, off-topic answer, unrelated fabrication, or equivalent fundamental failure.

## Important Evaluation Principles

### Accuracy

Central factual claims must be correct.

Minor factual imperfections should be judged according to whether they materially affect the reasoning or conclusion.

### Reasoning over recall

Correct facts without appropriate reasoning should not automatically receive a high score when the question requires analysis.

### Evidence

Answers should distinguish stronger evidence from weaker evidence. Evidence should support the actual claim being made.

### Correlation is not causation

Association alone does not establish a causal relationship. Causal conclusions require appropriate evidence or clearly stated assumptions.

### Competing explanations

Where multiple explanations fit the evidence, strong answers should acknowledge relevant alternatives and, where useful, state what evidence would discriminate among them.

### Source quality

Sources should be assessed based on relevance, methodology, independence, expertise, provenance, incentives, and context. No source type should be treated as automatically infallible.

### Quantitative reasoning

Numerical reasoning should use the correct denominator, units, comparison, and method.

Do not confuse:
- percentage change with percentage points;
- relative changes with absolute changes;
- totals with rates;
- averages with distributions.

### Estimation

Approximate questions may have multiple valid answers. Reward sound decomposition, transparent assumptions, plausible magnitude, and useful sensitivity checks rather than exact agreement with one estimate.

### Historical interpretation

Distinguish documented facts from interpretations of causes, motives, or significance. Where historians can reasonably disagree, evaluate evidentiary support and coherence rather than conformity to one interpretation.

### Scientific reasoning

Scientific explanations should be consistent with available evidence and established mechanisms. Models and experiments should be judged within their assumptions, measurement limits, and domains of application.

### Multidisciplinary synthesis

Combining more domains does not automatically improve an answer. Relevant domains should be connected through coherent mechanisms and should not contradict one another.

### Practical decisions

Practical questions may have multiple valid answers. Evaluate objectives, constraints, assumptions, risks, uncertainty, and trade-offs rather than enforcing one preferred decision.

### Insufficient information

When the available information does not support a unique conclusion, recognizing this is a strength. Do not reward fabricated certainty.

### Ambiguity

Strong answers should identify material ambiguities rather than silently choosing an interpretation when different interpretations change the result.

### Uncertainty

Confidence should match evidence strength. When material, strong answers distinguish among **certain**, **highly supported**, **plausible**, **possible**, and **speculative**.

### No jargon worship

Technical terminology without correct reasoning should not receive a high score.

### No citation worship

Citations do not compensate for inaccurate facts, invalid reasoning, or poor evidence evaluation.

### No length worship

Longer answers should not score higher merely because they contain more information. Relevance and precision matter.

## Applying Conditional Criteria

A criterion marked conditional should be scored only when it is materially relevant to the question. For example, do not penalize a purely qualitative answer under Quantitative Correctness if no numerical reasoning is required.

When multiple conditional criteria apply, score each on the part of the answer it governs. A weakness in one criterion may also affect another only when the weakness truly propagates—for example, a wrong denominator may damage both Quantitative Correctness and Data Interpretation.

## Multidisciplinary Judgment

For questions involving several disciplines, evaluate whether the answer:
- identifies the relevant domains;
- connects them coherently;
- avoids contradictions across domains;
- distinguishes stronger from weaker evidence;
- recognizes assumptions and uncertainty;
- identifies material trade-offs;
- explains mechanisms; and
- identifies relevant missing information.

A response does not need to mention every conceivable discipline. Reward **relevant synthesis, not breadth for its own sake**.
