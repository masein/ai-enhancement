# Anthropology & Human Geography Evaluation Rubric

## Task

Score ONE answer to ONE Anthropology & Human Geography question from 0–4.

The question metadata establishes the relevant domain, intent, difficulty, geographic/cultural context, and expected reasoning style.

Accept different valid anthropological interpretations, spatial explanations, methodological approaches, and comparative analyses where the evidence permits them. Do not require a particular theoretical school, ethnographic vocabulary, map method, or wording unless specified. Do not reward unnecessary length, jargon, famous-scholar names, or citations.

## Critical Error

If `critical_anthropology_human_geography_error` applies:

**score = 0**

Otherwise evaluate the applicable criteria.

A critical error is a materially false central anthropological, demographic, archaeological, spatial, methodological, or geographic claim that fundamentally invalidates the answer or reverses the substantive conclusion. Minor arithmetic slips, small terminology mistakes, reasonable alternative interpretations, legitimate theoretical disagreements, cautious uncertainty, minor omissions, reasonable approximations, or justified scale choices are not automatically critical.

## Score Anchors

### 4

The answer is anthropologically and geographically accurate, directly addresses the question, uses relevant cultural, demographic, historical, archaeological, environmental, or spatial evidence appropriately, recognizes scale and context, distinguishes observation from inference, avoids stereotypes and deterministic claims, considers meaningful alternative explanations, and reaches a well-supported conclusion.

For ethnographic questions, it interprets evidence contextually without unjustified generalization. For demographic questions, it uses the correct quantities and denominators. For geographic questions, it reasons correctly about scale, spatial relationships, and aggregation. For archaeological questions, it respects context and evidential limits.

### 3

The answer is substantially correct and useful but contains one meaningful omission, minor conceptual imprecision, limited reasoning gap, non-central quantitative error, or insufficient discussion of context, scale, heterogeneity, or uncertainty. The central conclusion remains sound.

### 2

The answer is partly correct or directionally useful but has important omissions, weak cultural or spatial reasoning, significant demographic errors, overgeneralization, insufficient evidence handling, or poor treatment of alternative explanations. Meaningful correction would be required.

### 1

The answer contains a substantial anthropological or geographic misunderstanding, major non-critical quantitative error, inappropriate generalization, weak contextual reasoning, or badly incomplete analysis, but does not meet the critical-error threshold.

### 0

Critical Anthropology & Human Geography error, no answer, off-topic answer, unrelated fabrication, or equivalent fundamental failure.

## Criterion Application

Evaluate only criteria relevant to the question. An archaeology question need not discuss urban geography; a demographic question need not discuss kinship; an ethnographic question need not contain quantitative calculations. Conditional criteria should not penalize answers for omitting irrelevant material. However, when a conditional consideration is essential to correctness, failure on that issue should affect the score.

## Important Evaluation Principles

### Culture is not static
Cultural systems can change through migration, generational change, markets, political institutions, technology, environmental conditions, and internal debate.

### Cultures are heterogeneous
Do not treat a named cultural, ethnic, religious, or national group as internally uniform.

### Group patterns do not determine individuals
Population-level patterns must not be applied deterministically to individual people.

### Race and culture are not interchangeable
Social racial categories, cultural identities, ancestry, and biological population variation are analytically distinct.

### Context matters
Practices and meanings should be interpreted in their historical and social setting.

### Cultural relativism is not moral endorsement
Understanding local meaning does not require accepting every practice as ethically justified.

### Ethnographic depth is not statistical representativeness
Ethnography can provide rich contextual evidence without establishing population prevalence.

### Observation is not interpretation
Field evidence and researcher interpretation should be distinguished.

### Reflexivity matters
Researcher position can influence access, evidence, disclosure, and interpretation.

### Space is not just distance
Social relations, infrastructure, borders, networks, and institutions shape spatial interaction.

### Scale matters
Patterns visible at one scale may disappear or reverse at another.

### Counts are not rates
Spatial and demographic data often require appropriate normalization.

### Maps are representations
Projection, classification, boundaries, symbols, and aggregation affect interpretation.

### Spatial correlation is not causation
Geographic clustering does not establish a causal mechanism.

### Avoid ecological fallacy
Area-level relationships do not necessarily apply to individuals.

### Migration stock is not migration flow
These are different demographic quantities.

### Historical legacies matter without determining everything
Path dependence can constrain current outcomes without making them inevitable.

### Environment does not mechanically determine society
Environmental conditions interact with institutions, technology, culture, and resources.

### Material evidence requires context
Archaeological interpretation depends on provenance, association, preservation, disturbance, and site formation.

### Identity is contextual
Identity categories can be multiple, changing, and contested.

### Multiple explanations often coexist
Human social and spatial outcomes frequently result from interacting mechanisms.

### Qualitative and quantitative evidence are complementary
Neither automatically outranks the other.

### No theory worship
Do not reward allegiance to one anthropological or geographic school.

### No jargon worship
Technical terminology without correct reasoning should not receive high credit.

### No citation worship
Citations do not compensate for weak cultural, spatial, demographic, archaeological, or methodological reasoning.

## Ethnographic Answers

Strong answers should, where appropriate:
- identify what was observed;
- identify whose perspective is represented;
- distinguish participant accounts from researcher interpretation;
- recognize context and variation;
- avoid unjustified generalization; and
- consider alternative interpretations.

Do not treat one account as representative without supporting evidence.

## Demographic Answers

Strong answers should, where relevant:
- identify the correct population;
- use the correct denominator;
- distinguish stocks from flows;
- distinguish rates from counts;
- interpret age structure;
- identify migration, fertility, and mortality components; and
- recognize uncertainty.

## Spatial Answers

Strong answers should consider scale, spatial unit, distance, accessibility, networks, aggregation, normalization, and spatial dependence where relevant. Do not infer causation solely from geographic clustering.

## Map and GIS Answers

Strong answers should consider map purpose, variable type, normalization, projection, classification, spatial resolution, aggregation, and network structure where relevant. Do not treat a visually striking map as self-validating evidence.

## Archaeological Answers

Strong answers should consider context, stratigraphy, association, preservation, disturbance, dating uncertainty, and alternative explanations where relevant. Do not infer complex social identities from isolated objects without justification.

## Comparative Answers

Strong answers should define the dimensions of comparison, preserve local context, recognize historical differences, distinguish common mechanisms from superficial similarity, and avoid assuming cases are equivalent.

## Human–Environment Answers

Strong answers should integrate environmental conditions, livelihoods, technology, institutions, access to resources, history, and vulnerability where relevant. Do not use environmental determinism.

## High-Difficulty Integrative Reasoning

Difficulty-4 and difficulty-5 responses should be credited for explicitly handling interacting scales, confounding, spatial dependence, historical path dependence, cultural heterogeneity, sampling limitations, competing interpretations, measurement bias, and causal uncertainty when the question makes these relevant. Valid alternative interpretations should receive full credit when they fit the evidence and are clearly justified.

## Critical-Error Examples

Central errors that can trigger `score = 0` include: treating culture as biologically determined; presenting social racial categories as discrete biological races; making deterministic claims about individuals from group membership; fundamentally confusing migration stock with flow; using the wrong demographic denominator in a way that reverses interpretation; treating raw counts as rates; committing a central ecological fallacy; claiming spatial correlation proves causation; ignoring a scale effect that reverses the conclusion; overinterpreting disturbed archaeological context; generalizing an entire culture from one informant; treating cultures as historically unchanged; environmental determinism; inferring ethnicity directly from artifacts; equating cultural relativism with required moral endorsement; equating legal borders with cultural boundaries; claiming a projection preserves all spatial properties; or asserting one causal explanation when the supplied evidence supports several.
