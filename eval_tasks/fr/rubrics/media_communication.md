# Media & Communication Evaluation Rubric

## Task

Score **ONE answer to ONE Media & Communication question from 0–4**.

The question metadata establishes the relevant domain, intent, difficulty, media context, audience, acuity, and expected reasoning style.

Accept different valid message strategies, interpretations, campaign designs, crisis responses, journalistic decisions, and media-effect explanations where the supplied evidence permits them.

Do not require a particular communication theory, platform strategy, newsroom convention, or political viewpoint unless the question specifies one. Do not reward unnecessary length, jargon, platform buzzwords, or citations.

## Critical Error

If `critical_media_communication_error` applies:

**score = 0**

A critical Media & Communication error is a materially false central communication, journalistic, causal, quantitative, verification, ethical, political-neutrality, or media-system claim that fundamentally invalidates the answer or reverses the substantive conclusion.

Examples include inventing central evidence; presenting an unverified allegation as fact; treating repeated reports from one source as independent corroboration; reversing a conclusion through a major reach/impressions/denominator error; equating engagement with persuasion or impact; claiming correlation proves a media effect; claiming virality establishes truth; treating an official statement as incontrovertible despite contrary evidence; confirming uncertain breaking news; labeling merely unverified content false; ignoring decisive provenance; making unsupported algorithmic-causation claims; equating CTR with profitability; presenting partisan judgment or voter-direction as neutral analysis; endorsing deceptive sponsorship; treating crisis messaging as a substitute for corrective action; or giving false reassurance despite material uncertainty.

Do **not** classify minor terminology errors, small non-consequential arithmetic errors, reasonable alternative strategies, defensible audience segmentations, legitimate interpretation differences, minor omissions, cautious uncertainty, different defensible crisis sequences, reasonable ethical disagreement, or minor platform-specific imprecision as critical.

## Score Anchors

### 4

The answer is accurate, directly addresses the communication problem, identifies the relevant audience, message, medium, objective, and evidence, interprets communication metrics correctly, distinguishes exposure from effect, handles source verification appropriately, and reaches a well-supported conclusion.

For journalism questions, it separates verified fact from allegation and evaluates source quality.

For media-effects questions, it recognizes selection, confounding, and causal limitations.

For crisis questions, it balances speed, accuracy, actionability, uncertainty, and trust.

For political-communication questions, it remains neutral and analytical.

### 3

The answer is substantially correct and useful but contains one meaningful omission, minor conceptual imprecision, limited audience analysis, non-central quantitative error, or insufficient treatment of uncertainty or alternatives.

The central conclusion remains sound.

### 2

The answer is partly correct or directionally useful but has important omissions, weak source reasoning, significant metric errors, incomplete causal analysis, unrealistic communication assumptions, or inadequate ethical/contextual reasoning.

Meaningful correction would be required.

### 1

The answer contains a substantial media/communication misunderstanding, major non-critical measurement or verification error, weak audience reasoning, or badly incomplete analysis, but does not meet the critical-error threshold.

### 0

Critical Media & Communication error, no answer, off-topic answer, unrelated fabrication, or equivalent fundamental failure.

## Criterion Application

Evaluate only criteria relevant to the question.

A journalism question need not discuss advertising. A crisis-communication question need not discuss media ownership. A platform-metrics question need not contain rhetorical analysis.

Conditional criteria should not penalize answers for omitting irrelevant material. However, when a conditional consideration is essential to correctness, failure on that issue should affect the score.

## Communication Foundations

Communication is not transmission alone. Meaning depends on audience, context, interpretation, feedback, and medium. Strong answers distinguish the message sent from the message received, the intended meaning from the interpreted meaning, and clarity from persuasion.

## Message, Rhetoric, and Framing

Strong answers explain what a rhetorical or message-design choice does, for which audience, and with what evidence. Naming a device is not enough.

Frames emphasize some aspects of an issue and de-emphasize others; framing does not necessarily falsify information or determine audience opinion.

Persuasion depends on source, message, audience, and context. Exposure is not persuasion.

## Journalism Answers

Strong answers should consider, where relevant:

- the precise claim
- source access and expertise
- provenance
- corroboration and source dependence
- source incentives and conflicts
- documentary or primary evidence
- uncertainty
- public interest
- correction obligations

Journalism is not stenography. Official sources remain subject to verification. Anonymous is not automatically unreliable, and repetition is not independent verification.

News value is not truth. A dramatic or prominent event can be newsworthy while particular claims about it remain unverified.

Verification precedes certainty.

## Media-Effects Answers

Strong answers should:

- define exposure
- define the outcome
- identify selection and self-selection
- identify confounders
- distinguish correlation from causation
- consider reverse and reciprocal effects
- identify what evidence would improve causal inference

Correlation is not a media effect. People choose media based on prior attitudes, interests, demographics, networks, and availability.

Long-term media-effects hypotheses should not be turned into claims that one exposure immediately caused a change.

## PR and Crisis Answers

Strong answers should identify, where relevant:

- stakeholder
- immediate information need
- confirmed facts
- unknowns
- actionable guidance
- responsibility
- next-update process
- corrective action and remediation

Crisis communication requires both speed and accuracy. False certainty can damage trust and safety.

Communication cannot substitute for action. Reputation repair requires credible conduct as well as messaging.

Public relations is not merely publicity; it includes stakeholder relationships, trust, internal communication, media relations, campaigns, issues management, and evaluation.

## Advertising and Campaign Answers

Strong answers should connect:

**objective → audience → message → channel → timing → metric**

Do not evaluate a campaign solely by impressions, clicks, likes, or other engagement.

Distinguish:

- awareness
- recall
- comprehension
- persuasion
- conversion
- retention
- cost efficiency
- longer-term impact

Paid, earned, owned, social, interpersonal, and event channels can reinforce or contradict one another. Audience overlap can create double counting.

## Social and Platform Answers

Strong answers should consider, where relevant:

- content properties
- audience behavior
- network structure
- ranking and recommendation
- moderation and governance
- platform incentives
- measurement limits

Algorithms are not sole causes. Platform outcomes emerge from interaction among ranking systems, users, content, networks, and incentives.

Engagement is not impact. Likes, clicks, comments, shares, watch time, and saves do not by themselves reveal beliefs, attitudes, trust, motives, or real-world behavior.

Virality is not credibility.

## Misinformation Answers

Strong answers should:

- identify the precise claim
- determine whether it is false, misleading, unverified, satirical, or manipulated
- trace provenance
- seek primary evidence
- distinguish independent corroboration from repetition
- preserve uncertainty
- propose proportionate correction when relevant

Corrections should make the corrected fact salient and should account for whether the correction reaches the same audience as the original claim.

## Political-Communication Answers

Strong answers should:

- describe communication strategy neutrally
- identify audience and framing
- separate factual claims from rhetoric
- analyze evidence and institutional context
- avoid endorsement
- avoid best/worst rankings
- avoid voter-direction
- avoid independent election prediction

Neutrality is not false equivalence. Fairness does not require treating unsupported claims as equally credible.

## Quantitative Answers

Strong answers should:

- choose the metric that matches the objective
- use the correct numerator and denominator
- distinguish reach, impressions, and frequency
- avoid double counting overlapping audiences
- perform calculations correctly
- recognize attribution limitations
- interpret the number in relation to the communication goal

A high metric is not automatically desirable if it is misaligned with the objective.

## Research-Method Answers

Strong answers should identify, where relevant:

- research question
- sample
- unit of analysis
- exposure measure
- outcome measure
- reliability
- validity
- confounders
- randomization or assignment mechanism
- spillovers
- nonresponse or recall problems
- uncertainty

Self-reported media use can be inaccurate. A measure of exposure is not necessarily a measure of attention, comprehension, trust, persuasion, or behavior.

## Ethics and Public Interest

Strong answers weigh truthfulness, accuracy, independence, harm, dignity, privacy, consent, manipulation, transparency, sponsorship, vulnerable audiences, and public interest when relevant.

Persuasion and manipulation differ. Deception, hidden sponsorship, coercion, transparency, and audience autonomy matter.

Photojournalism and visual reporting can involve genuine trade-offs; no single slogan resolves every case.

## Visual Communication and Data

Visual design can clarify or distort evidence. Consider axes, scale, labels, normalization, cropping, sequencing, captions, visual hierarchy, and context.

A misleading design does not necessarily make the underlying data false; the task is to distinguish the numeric evidence from the visual interpretation.

## Cross-Cultural and Global Communication

Treat cultural frameworks as hypotheses, not deterministic rules. Distinguish recurring communication patterns from individual behavior.

Global distribution does not guarantee uniform local interpretation.

## Core Evaluation Principles

- **Communication is not transmission alone.**
- **Exposure is not persuasion.**
- **Engagement is not impact.**
- **Virality is not credibility.**
- **Correlation is not a media effect.**
- **Selection matters.**
- **Algorithms are not sole causes.**
- **News value is not truth.**
- **Official is not automatically correct.**
- **Anonymous is not automatically unreliable.**
- **Verification precedes certainty.**
- **Corrections should be proportional and clear.**
- **Public relations is not merely publicity.**
- **Communication cannot substitute for action.**
- **Crisis communication requires both speed and accuracy.**
- **Political communication should be evaluated neutrally.**
- **Media representation requires evidence.**
- **Audience metrics have limits.**
- **Frames emphasize rather than necessarily falsify.**
- **Persuasion and manipulation differ.**
- **Data visualization can persuade.**
- **Platform-scale communication creates measurement problems.**
- **Journalism is not stenography.**
- **Neutrality is not false equivalence.**
- **No framework worship:** theories are analytical tools, not answers.
- **No jargon worship:** terms such as framing, engagement, virality, and authenticity must be tied to mechanisms.
- **No citation worship:** citations do not compensate for incorrect verification, metrics, or causal reasoning.

## Judgment and Valid Alternatives

Open-ended questions need not have one predetermined conclusion when multiple interpretations or strategies are compatible with the evidence.

A strong answer identifies the objective, audience, medium, evidence, source quality, measurement limits, selection/confounding, alternatives, ethics, institutional/platform context, and uncertainty **when those considerations are relevant**.

A technically and ethically justified alternative strategy or interpretation can receive full credit.
