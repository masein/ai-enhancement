# Arts Evaluation Rubric

## Task

Score **ONE answer to ONE Arts question from 0–4**.

The question metadata establishes the relevant medium, domain, intent, difficulty, historical/cultural context, and expected reasoning style. Accept different valid artistic interpretations, curatorial decisions, performance choices, critical evaluations, cross-media translations, and attributions where the supplied evidence permits them.

Do not require a particular art-historical school, aesthetic preference, performance interpretation, or terminology unless specified. Do not reward unnecessary length, jargon, artist-name dropping, movement-name dropping, or citations.

## Critical Error

If `critical_arts_error` applies:

**score = 0**

A critical Arts error is a materially false central formal, historical, technical, musical, performance, cinematic, conservation, attribution, provenance, or interpretive claim that fundamentally invalidates the answer or reverses its substantive conclusion.

Examples include inventing decisive visual or musical evidence, fundamentally confusing provenance with authorship, reversing a supplied film edit or sound relationship, applying an explicitly inappropriate analytical system, or recommending a destructive irreversible treatment when preservation is the task.

Do **not** treat as critical: minor terminology errors, legitimate alternative interpretations, reasonable aesthetic disagreements, valid staging choices, small chronology slips that do not affect reasoning, cautious uncertainty, legitimate attribution uncertainty, or defensible curatorial differences.

## Score Anchors

### 4

The answer is artistically, historically, and technically accurate; directly addresses the question; identifies relevant formal or performative evidence; explains how the relevant medium creates meaning; integrates context appropriately; distinguishes observation from interpretation; and reaches a well-supported conclusion.

For visual-art questions, it correctly analyzes form, material, composition, and context where relevant. For music questions, it correctly interprets supplied musical evidence and explains musical relationships. For theatre/dance questions, it distinguishes text or choreographic material from performance realization. For film questions, it integrates cinematography, editing, sound, performance, and mise-en-scène where relevant. For conservation/provenance questions, it correctly identifies evidential and ethical uncertainty.

### 3

The answer is substantially correct and useful but contains one meaningful omission, minor arts imprecision, limited evidence use, non-central historical/technical error, or insufficient treatment of an alternative interpretation. The central analysis remains sound.

### 2

The answer is partly correct or directionally useful but has important omissions, weak formal reasoning, significant historical or technical problems, unsupported interpretation, or inadequate understanding of the medium. Meaningful correction would be required.

### 1

The answer contains a substantial Arts misunderstanding, major non-critical formal or historical error, weak evidence use, or badly incomplete interpretation, but does not meet the critical-error threshold.

### 0

Critical Arts error, no answer, off-topic answer, unrelated fabrication, or equivalent fundamental failure.

## Criterion Application

Evaluate only criteria relevant to the question. A music question need not discuss painting technique; a conservation question need not analyze choreography; a theatre question need not discuss provenance unless relevant. Conditional criteria should not penalize answers for omitting irrelevant material. However, when a conditional consideration is essential to correctness, failure on that issue should affect the score.

## Core Evaluation Principles

### Description is not interpretation
Strong answers distinguish what is visibly, audibly, materially, or structurally present from what it may mean.

### Device identification is not analysis
Naming contrast, montage, syncopation, iconography, abstraction, site-specificity, polyphony, or another feature is insufficient without explaining where it appears, how it functions, what evidence supports the claim, and what interpretive consequence follows.

### Formal evidence matters
Claims should be grounded, where relevant, in form, material, technique, composition, performance, sequence, sound, movement, and spatial organization.

### Context matters without replacing the artwork
Historical or cultural context can illuminate a work but should not substitute for analysis of the work itself.

### Medium matters
Painting, sculpture, music, film, theatre, dance, photography, installation, craft, design, and digital media create meaning through different material, spatial, and temporal possibilities.

### Artist intention is evidence, not absolute authority
Artist statements can inform interpretation but do not eliminate other supported readings.

### Reception changes
Artworks can acquire new meanings through later audiences, institutions, display contexts, and criticism.

### Style is not quality
Identifying a style or movement does not establish artistic merit.

### Realism is not inherently superior
Different artistic systems pursue different representational goals.

### Technical skill is not identical to artistic value
Virtuosity may matter without being the only criterion.

### Abstraction is not absence of meaning
Formal relationships can carry meaning without recognizable subject matter.

### Cultural systems differ
Do not judge all traditions through one culture's artistic conventions.

### Global coverage matters
Do not treat European art history as a universal developmental sequence.

### Performance varies
The same score, script, choreography, or composition can support multiple valid realizations.

### Documentation is not identical to ephemeral work
Photographs, recordings, and written accounts of performance preserve evidence, not necessarily the full event.

### Conservation involves trade-offs
Preservation, historical change, appearance, reversibility, material stability, and access may conflict.

### Restoration is interpretive
Returning an object to an assumed original state is not a neutral act.

### Provenance is not attribution
Ownership history and authorship are different questions.

### Attribution is probabilistic
Style, documents, materials, technical evidence, comparative works, and workshop practice should be integrated.

### Price is not artistic value
Market price and artistic or historical significance are distinct.

### Popularity is not quality
Audience size alone does not establish artistic significance.

### Art criticism requires criteria
Evaluative claims should explain the standards being applied.

### Multiple interpretations can be valid
Interpretations should be judged by evidence and coherence, not conformity to one expected reading.

### No movement-name worship
Naming Impressionism, Cubism, Baroque, Minimalism, Conceptual Art, or another movement does not replace analysis.

### No jargon worship
Arts terminology without correct reasoning should not receive high credit.

### No citation worship
Citations do not compensate for incorrect formal, historical, musical, technical, or interpretive reasoning.

## Visual-Art Answers

Strong answers should consider, where relevant: line, color, value, texture, space, composition, scale, material, technique, subject, site, and context. Do not reward merely listing formal elements; require **observable feature → formal relationship → perceptual or interpretive effect**.

## Art-Historical Answers

Strong answers should establish relevant chronology and regional context; use documentary, material, comparative, patronage, market, institutional, reception, or archival evidence as appropriate; distinguish documentation from inference; and avoid teleological development narratives or Eurocentric universalization.

## Music Answers

Strong answers should consider rhythm, meter, melody, harmony, texture, timbre, dynamics, form, performance, improvisation, technology, and social context where relevant. Distinguish rhythm, meter, and tempo; melody and harmony; notation from performance; and do not require a Western tonal framework for traditions where it is inappropriate.

## Theatre and Dance Answers

Strong answers should distinguish text, score, choreographic material, staging, and performance realization; analyze gesture, voice, movement, spatial organization, performer relationships, audience relation, temporal structure, and cultural context where relevant. A script or movement vocabulary does not determine one valid staging.

## Film Answers

Strong answers should consider framing, camera position and movement, cinematography, editing, sound, mise-en-scène, performance, sequence, documentary evidence, and animation-specific structure where relevant. Distinguish shot content from meaning produced by editing and do not reduce film interpretation to plot summary.

## Design Answers

Strong answers should consider purpose, audience, hierarchy, material/form, legibility, readability, usability, accessibility, communication, and constraints. Distinguish communicative effectiveness from aesthetic preference or visual complexity.

## Curatorial Answers

Strong answers should identify the curatorial objective and explain artwork or object selection, sequence, spatial relationships, audience, interpretive materials, accessibility, pacing, and conservation constraints where relevant. Multiple exhibition designs may earn full credit if well justified.

## Conservation and Provenance Answers

Strong answers should distinguish stabilization, restoration, original material, later alteration, ownership history, authorship, authenticity, attribution, and uncertainty. They should recognize irreversible intervention risks, evidentiary gaps, and the difference between scientific measurement and historical inference.

## Comparative Answers

Strong answers should identify comparable dimensions, analyze meaningful differences, preserve cultural and historical context, avoid assuming visual similarity equals shared meaning, and explain medium-specific effects.

## Open-Ended Arts Judgment

For open-ended questions, evaluate whether the answer identifies relevant evidence, uses disciplinary concepts correctly, recognizes medium and technique, distinguishes observation from interpretation, integrates context where relevant, considers audience and reception, acknowledges alternatives, avoids unsupported symbolism and cultural generalization, calibrates uncertainty, and justifies evaluative claims using explicit criteria.

A valid alternative artistic interpretation should receive full credit when well supported.
